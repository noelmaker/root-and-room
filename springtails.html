THE TINIEST GARDENERS

Meet the springtails.

Small enough to go unnoticed, springtails play an important role in the miniature ecosystems beneath our feet.

These tiny, six-legged arthropods help break down organic matter and graze on fungi and microorganisms. In suitable bioactive enclosures, they contribute to nutrient cycling and help manage some fungal growth.

FIELD NOTE / 001

Springtails belong to a group called Collembola. Despite their appearance, they are not insects in the strict modern taxonomic sense.

01 / GETTING TO KNOW SPRINGTAILS

Small creatures. Remarkable adaptations.

Often overlooked because of their size, springtails are among the tiny inhabitants that help keep natural ecosystems functioning. Their unique adaptations allow them to thrive in a surprising variety of environments.

01 / ANATOMY & BEHAVIOR

Built to Bounce

Springtails are tiny, wingless, six-legged arthropods. Many possess a forked appendage called a furcula, which folds beneath the body and releases to propel them into the air.

This remarkable jumping mechanism helps them escape potential threats. However, not all springtail species possess a functional furcula.

02 / NATURAL HABITAT

Life Beneath the Leaves

Springtails inhabit soil, leaf litter, moss, decaying wood, and many other environments around the world. Many species favor moist conditions.

They feed on fungi, microorganisms, algae, and decomposing organic matter, contributing to decomposition and nutrient cycling.

03 / COMMON VARIETIES

More Than Tiny White Dots

Springtails come in a variety of shapes, sizes, and colors, including white, gray, orange, pink, and black. Some have elongated bodies, while others have a more rounded appearance.

White springtails are commonly kept in bioactive enclosures, but different species may have different moisture, temperature, and culture requirements.

FIELD NOTE / 002

Not every tiny white creature in an enclosure is a springtail. Mites and other small arthropods can have a similar appearance. Observe their body shape, movement, and behavior before attempting to identify them.

02 / CULTURE & CARE

Growing a little colony.

Maintaining a separate springtail culture provides a convenient way to introduce these tiny decomposers into new enclosures and replenish existing populations. With suitable moisture, food, and environmental conditions, a small culture can develop into a thriving colony.

METHOD / 001

Charcoal Cultures

Charcoal is a popular culture medium for commonly kept white springtails. Its porous structure provides surfaces for springtails to gather and feed.

  • Secure container with suitable ventilation
  • Plain horticultural charcoal without additives
  • A shallow reservoir of suitable water
  • Small amounts of springtail food

Keep the charcoal moist, with exposed surfaces above the water. Many commonly cultured white springtails can be harvested by adding water and gently pouring off floating individuals.

METHOD / 002

Soil-Based Cultures

Soil-based cultures provide a more natural environment and may be particularly suitable for species that prefer living within organic substrate.

  • Secure container with suitable ventilation
  • Pesticide-free organic substrate
  • Consistent substrate moisture
  • Suitable organic matter and supplemental food

Maintain damp rather than saturated substrate. Springtails can be transferred by moving small portions of populated substrate into another enclosure.

01 / MOISTURE

Keep It Moist

Check cultures regularly and replenish water as needed. Avoid allowing the culture medium to dry out completely.

02 / FEEDING

Small Offerings

Offer small amounts of suitable springtail food or nutritional yeast. Adjust portions according to consumption and avoid excessive food buildup.

03 / ENVIRONMENT

Stable Conditions

Keep cultures away from direct sunlight and excessive heat. Maintain appropriate temperatures and ventilation for the species you are keeping.

04 / HARVESTING

Leave Room to Grow

Harvest only a portion of the population at a time, allowing the remaining springtails to reproduce and maintain the original culture.

FIELD NOTE / 003

Consider maintaining a separate backup springtail culture. If a population declines or an enclosure needs replenishing, an established backup culture can help you avoid starting over.

03 / BIOACTIVE LIVING

A tiny part of something bigger.

Beneath the leaves and among the roots, springtails quietly contribute to the balance of a living ecosystem.

Alongside isopods, fungi, bacteria, and other soil organisms, springtails help recycle organic matter and support the natural processes that make a bioactive enclosure function.

FIELD NOTE / 004

A cleanup crew is part of a living ecosystem, not a replacement for responsible enclosure maintenance. Springtails help manage some fungal growth, but they cannot correct unsuitable habitat conditions.

01 / INTRODUCING SPRINGTAILS

Seeding Your Enclosure

Introduce springtails into suitable moist areas of the enclosure, such as beneath leaf litter, near moss, or around plant roots.

Transfer individuals from a charcoal culture using a small amount of water, or introduce a portion of populated substrate from a soil-based culture. Avoid adding more water than the enclosure can accommodate.

02 / WORKING TOGETHER

Springtails & Isopods

Isopods help break down larger pieces of organic material, while springtails graze on fungi and other microscopic food sources.

Together with other decomposers, they contribute to nutrient cycling and the breakdown of organic matter. Choose species with compatible environmental requirements.

03 / LONG-TERM BALANCE

Supporting the Population

Maintain suitable moisture, organic matter, and sheltered microhabitats where springtails can feed and reproduce.

Population sizes may naturally fluctuate as food and environmental conditions change. If numbers decline, investigate the habitat conditions before introducing more springtails.
