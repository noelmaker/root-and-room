Why Are My Calathea Leaves Curling?

Calatheas are known for their beautiful patterned foliage and leaves that move throughout the day. But when those leaves begin curling inward, rolling into tubes, or refusing to unfurl, it's natural to wonder what's wrong. From watering and humidity to light and temperature, here's how to identify seven possible causes and help your plant recover.

Root & Room Plant Care · 7 min read
The quick answer

Calathea leaves may curl because of underwatering, low humidity, excessive sunlight, temperature stress, root problems, or pests. However, Calatheas also naturally raise and lower their leaves throughout the day. If the leaves remain tightly curled, check the soil moisture, humidity, and growing conditions before changing your care routine.

Calatheas are famous for their beautiful patterned leaves, but they're also known for being a little particular about their growing conditions. When those colorful leaves begin curling inward or rolling into narrow tubes, it's easy to assume something has gone wrong.

Before making changes, it's important to understand whether your Calathea is moving its leaves naturally or curling because of environmental stress.

1. It may be normal nighttime leaf movement

Calatheas belong to a group of plants often called prayer plants because their leaves naturally change position throughout the day.

This movement, known as nyctinasty, often causes the leaves to become more upright during the evening and lower again during daylight hours.

However, normal nighttime movement is different from leaves curling tightly inward or remaining rolled up throughout the day.

Normal movement or a problem?

  • Leaves become more upright in the evening
  • Leaves return to their usual position during the day
  • Foliage remains firm and healthy-looking
  • No new browning, yellowing, or wilting appears
  • Leaves are not persistently rolled into tight tubes

What to do

Observe your Calathea at different times of day before changing its care routine. If the leaves are simply moving between daytime and nighttime positions, there's usually nothing to fix.

If the leaves stay tightly curled or begin developing additional symptoms, investigate the possible causes below.

2. The soil is becoming too dry

Calatheas generally prefer lightly and consistently moist potting mix. When the soil becomes excessively dry, their leaves may curl inward as the plant experiences water stress.

Dry conditions can also contribute to drooping, crispy edges, and browning leaf tips.

Root & Room Tip

Check the soil below the surface before watering. If the potting mix is very dry, water thoroughly and allow excess moisture to drain away. Avoid letting the pot sit in standing water.

Going forward, aim for consistent moisture without keeping the roots constantly saturated.

3. The indoor air is too dry

Calatheas naturally grow in humid tropical environments, and very dry indoor air can contribute to curling leaves and crispy edges.

Heating systems, air conditioning, and dry seasonal conditions can reduce indoor humidity, especially during colder months.

Look more closely if...

  • Leaves remain curled despite appropriate soil moisture
  • Leaf edges are becoming brown or crispy
  • The plant sits near a heating or cooling vent
  • Indoor humidity is consistently low
  • Several leaves are developing similar symptoms

What to do

Move your Calathea away from heating vents and strong drafts. If your home is particularly dry, a humidifier can help maintain more consistent humidity.

Avoid assuming humidity is the only problem. Underwatering, excessive light, and root stress can produce similar symptoms.

4. The plant is receiving too much sunlight

Calatheas generally prefer bright, indirect light or filtered light. Strong direct sunlight can stress their delicate foliage, especially during the hottest part of the day.

Excessive light and heat can increase water loss from the leaves, sometimes contributing to curling, faded patterns, or brown, crispy patches.

Look more closely if...

  • Leaves curl during the sunniest part of the day
  • Foliage develops pale or bleached patches
  • Brown areas appear on sun-exposed leaves
  • The plant sits in strong afternoon sunlight

What to do

Move your Calathea away from harsh direct sunlight or use a sheer curtain to filter the light.

Avoid moving it into a very dark location. The goal is to provide enough indirect light for healthy growth without exposing the foliage to intense sun.

5. Overwatering or root damage

Although Calatheas prefer consistent moisture, their roots still need oxygen. Potting mix that remains saturated for too long can contribute to root deterioration.

Damaged roots may struggle to absorb water properly, causing leaves to curl or wilt even when the soil feels wet.

Yellowing foliage, declining growth, and persistently wet soil may accompany root problems.

Possible root trouble

  • Leaves remain curled despite wet soil
  • Several leaves are yellowing or wilting
  • The potting mix stays saturated for days
  • The container has inadequate drainage
  • Roots appear soft, mushy, or deteriorated

What to do

Check the soil moisture and make sure excess water can drain freely. Avoid leaving the pot standing in collected water.

If your Calathea continues declining, carefully inspect the roots. Healthy roots should generally feel firm rather than mushy.

Don't automatically add more water to a curling plant without checking the soil first.

6. Temperature changes or drafts

Calatheas are tropical plants that generally prefer stable, warm indoor conditions.

Cold drafts, sudden temperature changes, or direct airflow from heating and cooling systems can stress their foliage.

If curling began after moving your plant, opening a nearby window during cold weather, or changing your home's heating routine, consider temperature stress as a possible cause.

Root & Room Tip

Choose a location with stable temperatures and keep your Calathea away from exterior doors, cold windows, and heating or cooling vents.

7. Pests may be damaging the foliage

Spider mites, thrips, and other common houseplant pests can damage Calathea leaves.

Feeding damage may cause distorted growth, pale speckling, discoloration, or curling foliage.

Spider mites can be especially troublesome in warm, dry indoor environments. Fine webbing may become visible as an infestation develops.

Signs to investigate

  • Tiny pale or yellow speckles on leaves
  • Fine webbing beneath or between leaves
  • Distorted or damaged new growth
  • Visible insects or tiny moving pests
  • Curling accompanied by unusual discoloration

What to do

Inspect both sides of the leaves, especially the undersides and areas where new growth emerges.

If you find pests, separate the affected plant from nearby houseplants while identifying the problem.

Choose a treatment appropriate for the pest and follow the product label carefully. Avoid applying treatments unnecessarily when no pests are present.

Can curled Calathea leaves uncurl?

Yes, Calathea leaves that have curled because of temporary environmental stress may return to a more normal position once conditions improve.

For example, leaves that curled because the soil became too dry may begin relaxing after the plant receives adequate moisture.

However, leaves that have developed significant physical damage, extensive browning, or dead tissue may not fully recover.

Healthy new growth is often a better indicator of recovery than the appearance of previously damaged leaves.

How long does it take Calathea leaves to uncurl?

Recovery depends on what caused the curling and how severely the plant was affected.

Leaves curled by temporary underwatering may begin relaxing within several hours to a few days after the potting mix is properly rehydrated.

If the problem involves root damage, prolonged environmental stress, or pests, improvement may take considerably longer.

Some damaged leaves may never fully return to their original appearance, even when the plant begins recovering.

Rather than expecting an exact recovery timeline, watch for reduced curling, stable foliage, and healthy new leaves.

Should you cut off curled Calathea leaves?

Curling alone isn't a reason to remove a Calathea leaf.

If the leaf remains green and otherwise healthy, it may recover once the underlying problem is corrected.

Removing healthy foliage unnecessarily can reduce the plant's ability to produce energy.

If a leaf has become completely brown, dried out, or severely damaged, you can remove it using clean, sharp scissors.

Cut the leaf stalk carefully near its base without damaging surrounding growth.

When should you worry about curling Calathea leaves?

Leaves that simply become more upright at night usually aren't a cause for concern.

However, persistent curling throughout the day, especially when accompanied by browning, yellowing, wilting, or damaged new growth, deserves closer attention.

Check the soil moisture, humidity, light exposure, and temperature.

If the soil remains wet while the plant continues declining, investigate possible root problems.

Also inspect the foliage for pests if curling appears alongside unusual spotting, fine webbing, or distorted growth.

Avoid changing several parts of your care routine at once. Identifying the most likely cause first makes it easier to see whether your adjustments are helping.
