Why Is My Snake Plant Drooping?

Snake plant leaves are supposed to stand tall, so a leaf suddenly leaning, bending, or collapsing deserves a closer look. Here's how to figure out what's happening.

Root & Room Plant Care · 7 min read
The quick answer

A drooping snake plant can be caused by excess water and unhealthy roots, severe dryness, inadequate growing conditions, physical damage, or an older leaf simply losing its structure. Start with the soil and roots before assuming your plant needs more water.

Snake plants have a reputation for surviving almost anything. Unfortunately, that reputation sometimes convinces us that they can't have problems at all.

Healthy snake plant leaves are normally thick and upright. When one starts leaning or an entire plant begins flopping over, don't immediately reach for the watering can. First look at the soil, the base of the leaves, the roots, and which leaves are actually drooping.

1. The soil is staying too wet

This is the first thing worth checking.

Snake plants tolerate dry conditions far better than constantly wet potting mix. When their roots remain wet for too long, root rot can develop. Damaged roots can no longer support the plant properly, and leaves may begin to wilt or collapse.

Signs to look for

  • Potting mix that remains wet for a long time
  • Soft or mushy areas near the base of a leaf
  • Yellowing along with drooping
  • Dark, soft or unhealthy-looking roots
  • A container without adequate drainage

What to do

Let the potting mix dry between waterings and make sure excess water can escape through drainage holes.

If the plant is declining while the soil remains wet, inspect the roots. Healthy roots should be firm rather than dark, soft and rotten.

2. The plant has become extremely dry

Yes, the plant famous for surviving neglect can eventually become too dry.

Snake plants store water in their thick foliage and tolerate prolonged dry periods, so they usually don't need frequent watering. But extended drought can eventually cause plant tissue to lose firmness and show stress.

Root & Room Tip

Don't diagnose drooping by appearance alone. Feel the soil. Drooping in soggy soil and drooping in bone-dry soil call for very different solutions.

3. It's growing in very little light

Snake plants are genuinely tolerant of low-light rooms, which is one reason they're such popular houseplants.

But “tolerates low light” doesn't mean that more light is useless. Plants growing with very little available light grow more slowly and use water more slowly, which can also make overwatering easier.

If possible, give your snake plant useful indirect light while avoiding a sudden move from a dark location into intense direct sun.

4. One leaf has been physically damaged

If the rest of the plant is standing perfectly upright and you're staring at one bent leaf, consider the wonderfully unexciting possibility that something bumped it.

Tall snake plant leaves can be bent by pets, people, moving furniture, transportation, or simply being squeezed into an awkward location.

A sharply creased or broken leaf generally won't straighten itself back into its original shape.

5. The plant is becoming crowded or unstable

Snake plants grow from underground rhizomes and can eventually form a very dense clump.

They don't mind being somewhat snug, but an extremely crowded plant can become physically unstable. Tall varieties can also become top-heavy enough to tip a lightweight container.

Check the pot if...

  • The plant is extremely crowded
  • Roots or rhizomes are distorting the container
  • The entire plant feels unstable rather than wilted
  • Tall leaves are making a lightweight pot tip over

6. The roots are unhealthy

Sometimes the leaves only tell you that something is wrong below the soil.

If your snake plant is drooping even though the watering situation doesn't make sense, gently inspect the root system. Root rot can prevent a plant from taking up water effectively even while the surrounding soil is wet.

Look for roots that are dark, soft or deteriorating rather than firm and healthy.

7. An older outer leaf is declining

One leaning outer leaf doesn't necessarily mean the entire plant is failing.

Older or damaged leaves can eventually decline while the center of the plant continues producing healthy new growth. Look at the plant as a whole before making major changes.

If the newest growth is upright and healthy and the problem is limited to one old outer leaf, aggressive intervention may not be necessary.

Can a drooping snake plant stand back up?

It depends on why the leaf is drooping.

A plant that has temporarily lost firmness because of water stress may improve after the underlying problem is corrected. A leaf that has become badly rotted, creased or physically broken, however, isn't likely to rebuild damaged tissue and become perfectly upright.

Judge recovery by the condition of the plant and its new growth, not by expecting every damaged old leaf to repair itself.

Should you cut off a drooping snake plant leaf?

You don't have to remove a healthy leaf simply because it leans a little.

But if a leaf is badly damaged, rotting or collapsing at the base, it can be removed with a clean cutting tool. Cut the damaged leaf near the soil line without injuring neighboring growth.

More importantly, determine why it failed. Removing a rotten leaf without correcting persistently wet soil won't solve the underlying problem.
