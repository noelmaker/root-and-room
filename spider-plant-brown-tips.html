Why Does My Spider Plant Have Brown Tips?

Those crispy little ends are common — but they can happen for several different reasons. Here's how to narrow down what's bothering your spider plant.

Root & Room Plant Care · 7 min read
The quick answer

Brown spider plant tips can be associated with inconsistent watering, dry conditions, mineral or fertilizer salt buildup, water quality, or too much direct sun. A few brown tips don't necessarily mean the whole plant is unhealthy — look at the soil, growing conditions, and pattern of damage before changing its care.

Spider plants have a slightly annoying habit: the plant can look perfectly healthy, be growing new leaves and babies, and still develop crispy brown tips.

That's because a brown tip is a symptom rather than a diagnosis. Before changing everything about your plant's routine, look at when the browning started and what the rest of the plant is doing.

1. Minerals or salts are building up

Spider plants can develop tip burn when soluble salts accumulate in their potting mix. Those salts can come from fertilizer or, depending on your water supply, minerals in irrigation water.

If the plant otherwise looks healthy but many leaves repeatedly develop brown ends, salt buildup is worth considering.

Clues to look for

  • Brown, dry tips on several otherwise healthy leaves
  • A white or crusty residue around the soil or pot
  • Frequent fertilizer use
  • The problem repeatedly returns after trimming the tips

What to do

Make sure excess water can drain freely from the container. Periodic thorough watering can help move accumulated soluble salts through the potting mix rather than allowing them to continually build up.

If you've been fertilizing frequently, pause and reconsider how much the plant actually needs. More fertilizer isn't automatically better.

2. Your watering routine is inconsistent

Spider plants prefer fairly even moisture during active growth, although the soil shouldn't remain constantly saturated.

Repeatedly allowing the plant to become extremely dry and then soaking it can contribute to stressed foliage and brown tips.

Root & Room Tip

Don't water because Tuesday is “watering day.” Check the soil first. Your plant doesn't own a calendar.

3. The air is very dry

Dry indoor conditions can contribute to browning along leaf tips and margins, particularly when several stressors are happening at once.

This can become more noticeable when indoor heating is running and the air around your plants becomes considerably drier.

You don't necessarily need to turn your house into a tropical greenhouse. Start by keeping the plant away from especially hot, drying drafts and making sure its basic watering needs are being met.

4. Your water may be contributing

Spider plants are often discussed as being sensitive to certain dissolved minerals and salts in water. The important part is not to assume tap water is automatically the culprit before checking the easier possibilities.

If your watering routine, light and fertilizer use all seem reasonable but brown tips persist, experimenting with a different water source for a while can help you see whether water quality is contributing.

5. It's getting too much direct sun

Spider plants grow well in bright, indirect light. Strong direct sunlight can damage foliage, especially when a plant that was previously growing in lower light is suddenly moved into intense sun.

Sun damage may appear as bleached, tan or brown areas rather than being perfectly confined to the very ends of the leaves.

Think about light if...

  • Damage is strongest on the window-facing side
  • Leaves have bleached or scorched patches as well as brown areas
  • You recently moved the plant into stronger sunlight
  • The plant sits in intense direct afternoon sun

6. The roots are crowded or stressed

Spider plants develop thick, fleshy roots and can eventually become very crowded in their containers.

Being somewhat snug isn't automatically an emergency, but a severely crowded root system can make watering more difficult because there may be relatively little potting mix left to hold moisture.

If water races straight through the pot, the soil dries unusually quickly, or roots are tightly filling the container, it may be time to inspect the root system.

7. The damaged tips are simply old damage

Here's an easy one to overlook: brown tissue doesn't turn green again.

You may have already corrected whatever caused the problem, but the old brown tips will remain on those leaves. Instead of judging the plant by old damage, watch the new growth.

If new leaves are emerging healthy, that's a much better indication of whether your changes are working.

Should you cut the brown tips off a spider plant?

You can. Brown leaf tissue is already damaged and won't become green again, so trimming it is mostly a cosmetic choice.

Use clean scissors and trim only the dead brown portion rather than cutting deeply into healthy green tissue. You can follow the natural pointed shape of the leaf so the trim is less noticeable.

Do brown tips mean your spider plant is dying?

Not necessarily. A plant with a few brown tips can still be actively producing healthy leaves and plantlets.

Pay more attention if browning is spreading rapidly, entire leaves are declining, the plant is wilting despite moist soil, or the roots appear unhealthy. Those signs suggest a larger problem than a few cosmetic brown ends.
