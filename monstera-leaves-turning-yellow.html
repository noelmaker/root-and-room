Why Are My Monstera Leaves Turning Yellow?

One yellow leaf doesn't always mean disaster. The pattern, soil, roots, and age of the leaf can help you figure out what your Monstera is trying to tell you.

Root & Room Plant Care · 7 min read
The quick answer

Yellow Monstera leaves can be associated with excess moisture and root problems, very dry conditions, unsuitable light, pests, soluble-salt stress, or the natural decline of an older leaf. Don't diagnose the plant from color alone — check the soil, roots, leaf pattern, and new growth before changing its care.

A yellow Monstera leaf has an annoying number of possible explanations. That's why “yellow leaf = needs water” is a particularly dangerous shortcut.

Instead, treat yellowing like a clue. Start with where the yellow leaves are, how quickly the change happened, and whether the soil is wet or dry.

1. The soil is staying too wet

This is one of the first possibilities worth checking, especially if several leaves are yellowing while the potting mix remains wet.

Monstera prefers well-draining potting mix and should not remain continuously saturated. Excess moisture can deprive roots of healthy growing conditions and contribute to root rot.

Look more closely if...

  • The soil stays wet for a long time after watering
  • Several leaves are yellowing rather than one old leaf
  • The plant is wilting even though the soil is moist
  • The pot has poor or nonexistent drainage
  • Roots look dark, brown, soft, or mushy

What to do

Check the potting mix before watering again. Penn State recommends watering Monstera thoroughly and then allowing the top 1 to 2 inches of soil to dry before the next watering.

If the plant is declining while the soil remains wet, inspect the roots rather than simply waiting for the foliage to recover.

2. The plant has become too dry

The opposite problem can also stress foliage.

A Monstera that repeatedly becomes extremely dry may begin showing stressed leaves and slower growth. Soil that has become severely dry can sometimes pull away from the sides of the container, making it harder to rehydrate evenly.

Root & Room Tip

Check the soil before reacting to a yellow leaf. Yellowing in soggy soil and yellowing in severely dry soil require very different responses.

3. The roots are beginning to rot

Root rot deserves its own section because the symptoms above the soil can be confusing.

A plant with damaged roots may yellow or wilt even when moisture is available. That's because unhealthy roots can no longer function normally.

Healthy roots should generally feel firm. Roots affected by rot may become brown to black, soft, mushy, or deteriorated.

Possible root trouble

  • Yellowing is spreading
  • The plant looks wilted despite moist soil
  • Growth has slowed significantly
  • The base of the plant looks dark or soft
  • Roots are brown, black, or mushy instead of firm

4. It isn't getting enough useful light

Monstera can tolerate a range of indoor conditions, but inadequate light affects how actively the plant grows.

Lower light also means the plant generally uses water more slowly. That can create an indirect problem: a watering routine that worked in brighter conditions may keep the potting mix wet for much longer after the plant is moved somewhere darker.

Look at light and watering together rather than treating them as completely separate parts of plant care.

5. The plant may be dealing with pests

If yellowing comes with speckling, webbing, sticky residue, cottony material, bumps, or visible insects, inspect the plant carefully for pests.

Monstera can be affected by common indoor pests including spider mites, mealybugs, scale insects, aphids, and thrips.

Check both sides of the leaves, petioles, stems, and the places where leaves join the plant rather than inspecting only the upper leaf surface.

6. Fertilizer or soluble salts may be contributing

More fertilizer does not automatically produce a healthier Monstera.

Soluble salts can accumulate in container potting mix from fertilizer and other sources. Indoor-plant diagnostic guidance includes high soluble salts among possible causes of yellowing, browning, or wilting foliage.

If you've been fertilizing frequently or notice crusty deposits around the soil or container, consider whether buildup may be contributing before adding another dose.

7. It's simply an older leaf

This is the possibility that saves everyone from repotting a perfectly healthy plant at 11 p.m.

Individual older leaves eventually decline. If one of the oldest lower leaves slowly turns yellow while the rest of the Monstera is healthy and producing good new growth, normal leaf aging may be the explanation.

The important distinction is the pattern. One aging lower leaf is very different from several leaves rapidly yellowing throughout the plant.

Should you cut yellow leaves off a Monstera?

A fully yellow leaf won't turn green again, so removing it is usually a cosmetic choice once the leaf is declining.

Use a clean cutting tool and remove the leaf without damaging neighboring stems or new growth.

But don't let pruning become the “solution.” If multiple leaves are yellowing, figure out why before simply cutting away the evidence.

Can a yellow Monstera leaf turn green again?

Once a leaf has lost substantial chlorophyll and become fully yellow, don't expect it to return to its original healthy green appearance.

Your better measure of recovery is what happens next. Watch whether the yellowing stops spreading and whether new growth emerges healthy.

When should you worry about yellow Monstera leaves?

Pay closer attention when yellowing is rapid, affects multiple leaves, appears alongside wilting or stem deterioration, or occurs while the potting mix remains persistently wet.

Those patterns suggest something broader than the ordinary decline of a single old leaf.
