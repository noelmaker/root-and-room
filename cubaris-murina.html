ROOT & ROT · ISOPOD CARE GUIDE

Cubaris murina

Little Sea Isopod · Papaya · Florida Orange & More

Small, round, and full of personality! Cubaris murina is one of my favorite isopod species, especially the adorable pink and peach-colored Papaya variety.

These little guys are relatively easy to care for once you get their enclosure set up properly. Whether you're starting your first colony or adding another variety to your collection, here's what you need to know to keep them happy and healthy.

KEEPING THEM HAPPY

The Basics

You don't need a complicated setup to keep these little guys happy. Here's what matters most!

01 / THEIR HOME

Bigger Is Better!

When choosing an enclosure, I recommend going a little bigger than you think you'll need! More floor space makes it easier to create moist, less moist, and drier areas so your isopods can move around and find what feels comfortable for them.

For a starter colony of around 10–20 isopods, I personally recommend an enclosure that's at least 11.8 × 7.8 × 5.9 inches (length × width × height), or larger. When it comes to isopods, I'd choose a longer enclosure over a taller one any day!

Add about 2–3 inches of suitable substrate, plenty of dried leaf litter, cork bark, and hiding spots. Make sure the enclosure has secure ventilation, too.

What I learned: I originally started with a 7.8 × 5.9 × 3.9-inch enclosure. With the number of isopods I had at the time, I found it difficult to maintain a properly drier area without affecting the moist side. After moving to a larger enclosure, I had much more room to manage the moisture gradient.

02 / MOISTURE

Damp, Not Drenched!

Keep one side of the enclosure damp with sphagnum moss and let the other side stay a little drier. This gives your isopods the freedom to move wherever they're most comfortable. Just don't let the entire enclosure dry out.

My little trick: I like using a small plastic dropper pipette to add water directly into the substrate along the sides of the enclosure. This helps me moisten the deeper layers without soaking everything on top. On the drier side, I only add a little water to the surface when needed, rather than watering all the way through.

03 / FEEDING

What's for Dinner?

Dried leaves and decaying hardwood are their everyday food, so keep those available at all times. You can also offer small amounts of vegetables and protein once or twice a week. Don't forget a calcium source, such as cuttlebone or an isopod calcium block!

My little trick: I like to keep a calcium block on the drier side of my enclosure so it doesn't stay damp and encourage mold growth. Once I started noticing babies hanging out mostly on the moist side, I added a few small pieces of calcium beneath their favorite lotus pod, too. I figured this way, they'd have calcium nearby without needing to travel across the enclosure to reach the larger block.

04 / BREEDING

And Then There Were Babies!

Once your colony settles in, you may start spotting tiny babies hiding beneath leaves, moss, or cork bark. Newly born isopods are often very pale and much smaller than the adults. Give your colony time, keep their conditions steady, and avoid disturbing their hiding spots too often.

What I've noticed: My baby Papayas were so tiny and white that I almost mistook them for springtails! I've also noticed that some of my adults seem to favor the narrow space between the substrate and enclosure wall, underneath the moss, around the time babies appear. I've seen similar behavior in my Dairy Cow colony, too. I can't say for sure whether they're choosing those spots to give birth, but it's something I've noticed in both colonies!

A LITTLE PERSONAL EXPERIENCE

A Note From My Colony

I currently keep the Papaya variety of Cubaris murina, and they're easily one of my favorites! I love their soft pink and peach coloring, and it's always fun peeking into their enclosure to see what they're up to.

Mine seem especially fond of their lotus seed pods, particularly the ones on the moist side of the enclosure. It's where I usually find most of the colony gathered together, tucked into all those little spaces.

Every colony is a little different, and I'm still learning from mine. That's part of what makes keeping these little creatures so interesting!
