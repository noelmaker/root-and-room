AT A GLANCE

The essentials, at a glance.

A thriving isopod colony depends on more than just a container. From a nourishing substrate and balanced moisture to natural shelter and proper nutrition, these six essentials form the foundation of responsible isopod care.

01 / ENCLOSURE Space & Ventilation

Choose a secure container with enough floor space for your colony and ventilation appropriate for the species.

02 / SUBSTRATE A Living Foundation

Use a nutrient-rich substrate containing organic matter that supports burrowing, moisture retention, and decomposition.

03 / MOISTURE Wet & Dry Retreats

Create a moisture gradient with a consistently damp refuge and a less moist area, adjusted to your species. Avoid saturated, stagnant soil.

04 / HIDING PLACES Natural Shelter

Add cork bark, decaying hardwood, and other safe natural materials to create sheltered spaces.

05 / LEAF LITTER Food & Ground Cover

Provide a generous layer of pesticide-free, dried hardwood leaves. They offer shelter and a long-lasting food source.

06 / NUTRITION Calcium & Supplements

Offer a calcium source and appropriate supplemental foods alongside the enclosure's natural decaying materials.

Every isopod species has its own preferences. Use these essentials as a starting point, then adjust moisture, ventilation, and habitat conditions to suit your colony.

HABITAT & ENVIRONMENT

Building their little world.

A thriving isopod habitat starts with more than a container. The right setup combines space, nutritious substrate, ventilation, natural shelter, and carefully managed moisture.

01 / THE ENCLOSURE

Choosing the Right Container

Plastic and glass enclosures can both work well for isopods. Look for a secure container with adequate floor space, room for substrate, and ventilation appropriate for the species.

Start with a manageable enclosure that allows you to maintain different moisture levels. As the colony grows, reassess its space, ventilation, and overall conditions.

02 / THE SUBSTRATE

Creating a Living Foundation

Substrate is more than bedding. It provides a place to burrow, supports beneficial decomposers, holds moisture, and contributes organic material to the habitat.

A suitable starting mixture may include organic soil, decomposing hardwood leaves, and rotten hardwood. Provide enough depth for burrowing and moisture retention, adjusting the mixture for your species.

03 / MOISTURE GRADIENT

Creating Wet & Dry Retreats

Most commonly kept terrestrial isopods need access to a moist refuge to support respiration and successful molting.

For species that benefit from a moisture gradient, keep one area consistently damp while allowing another to remain less moist. Avoid saturated, stagnant substrate and adjust conditions according to the species you keep.

04 / VENTILATION

Balancing Airflow & Humidity

Ventilation helps prevent stagnant conditions and allows fresh air to circulate through the habitat. However, excessive airflow can cause the enclosure to dry out.

Use appropriately sized, securely screened ventilation openings. Monitor moisture levels and adjust airflow and watering together to maintain suitable conditions.

05 / NATURAL SHELTER

Hiding Places & Leaf Litter

Cork bark, decaying hardwood, and dried hardwood leaves provide shelter and surfaces where isopods can gather, feed, and molt.

Cover much of the substrate with pesticide-free leaf litter. It serves as both ground cover and a staple food source. Replenish it as needed.

06 / ONGOING CARE

Maintaining a Healthy Habitat

A bioactive enclosure is a living system that requires observation. Check moisture levels, ventilation, food availability, and the condition of the substrate regularly.

Remove uneaten supplemental foods before they spoil, replenish leaf litter and calcium sources, and monitor for signs of overcrowding or unhealthy conditions.

MOISTURE & MICROCLIMATES

Finding the right balance.

Moisture is one of the most important parts of a successful isopod habitat. The goal is to create an environment where isopods can find the moisture they need without keeping the entire enclosure constantly wet.

UNDERSTANDING MOISTURE

Moist substrate is not the same as humid air.

Air humidity describes the amount of moisture in the air, while substrate moisture describes how much water is held within the soil and other enclosure materials.

Isopods depend on suitable moist microhabitats to avoid drying out. A humidity reading alone cannot tell you whether the substrate provides those conditions.

Many commonly kept terrestrial isopods benefit from a moisture gradient. This means maintaining a consistently damp refuge, often with sphagnum moss, alongside an area that is less moist.

Different species have different moisture requirements. Some prefer more consistently humid conditions, while others need a larger, comparatively dry area. Always adjust the enclosure to the species you keep.

01 / THE MOIST REFUGE

A Place to Rehydrate

Keep one portion of the substrate consistently moist, without allowing water to pool. Damp sphagnum moss and sheltered bark can help create a humid refuge.

Check moisture below the surface, since the top layer can appear dry while the substrate underneath remains damp.

02 / THE DRIER ZONE

Room to Choose

Provide a less damp area with leaf litter and hiding places. This allows isopods to move between microhabitats instead of being confined to uniformly wet conditions.

The drier side does not need to be completely bone-dry, particularly for young isopods and during molting.

03 / TEMPERATURE

Comfortable Conditions

Most commonly kept terrestrial isopods do well in stable, moderate temperatures, but the ideal range varies by species. Research the temperature requirements of your particular isopods before setting up their enclosure.

Keep enclosures away from direct sunlight, radiators, heating vents, and other sources of excessive heat. Monitor temperatures during seasonal changes and avoid sudden fluctuations.

OBSERVING YOUR COLONY

What should you watch for?

Potentially Too Wet

Standing water, saturated substrate, persistent condensation combined with poor airflow, or stagnant conditions may indicate excessive moisture or insufficient ventilation.

>

Potentially Too Dry

A completely dry moist refuge, brittle sphagnum moss, or substrate that remains dry beneath the surface may indicate insufficient moisture.

Behavioral Clues

Unusual clustering, repeated escape attempts, or unexpected deaths can signal environmental stress, but these signs have multiple possible causes.

Field note: Adjust moisture gradually and observe how the colony responds. Avoid making several major enclosure changes at the same time.

THE LIVING FOOD SYSTEM

Nourishing their little world.

A well-designed isopod enclosure does more than provide shelter. It also contains natural materials that gradually break down, supporting isopods and the microorganisms that share their habitat.

01 / THE FOUNDATION

Leaf Litter & Decaying Wood

Dried hardwood leaves and naturally decomposing hardwood form an important part of the enclosure's food system. As these materials break down, they support isopods and beneficial microorganisms.

Keep leaf litter available throughout the habitat and replenish it as it is consumed. Use materials from safe, pesticide-free sources.

02 / SUBSTRATE NUTRITION

Organic Matter Beneath the Surface

A nutritious substrate contains more than plain soil. Suitable decomposing leaves, rotten hardwood, and other safe organic materials help support the enclosure's natural food web.

Mix some organic material into the substrate rather than relying entirely on surface leaf litter. Avoid synthetic fertilizers, pesticides, and chemically treated materials.

03 / MINERAL SUPPORT

Providing Calcium

Isopods need calcium to develop and maintain their exoskeletons. A suitable calcium source should be available within the enclosure.

Cuttlebone and clean, crushed eggshells are commonly used options. Check these periodically and replenish them as needed.

04 / SUPPLEMENTAL FEEDING

Adding Variety Without Excess

Depending on the species, supplemental foods may include prepared isopod diets, appropriate protein sources, and small portions of pesticide-free vegetables.

Offer modest amounts and remove uneaten perishable food before it spoils. Supplemental feeding should support, not replace, the enclosure's natural food sources.

THE FEEDING FIELD GUIDE

A simple feeding rhythm.

ALWAYS AVAILABLE

Natural Food Sources

Keep dried hardwood leaf litter, suitable decaying wood, and a calcium source available.

REGULAR SUPPLEMENTS

Protein

Offer a small portion of suitable protein-rich food, adjusting the frequency to your species, colony size, and appetite. Remove leftovers before they spoil.

OCCASIONAL VARIETY

Vegetables

Offer a small piece occasionally. Remove uneaten portions before mold or decomposition becomes excessive.

EVERY FEEDING

Observe & Adjust

Check what was eaten, remove spoiled food, and adjust future portions to the colony's needs.

FIELD NOTE / 003

More food is not always better. Small, consistent offerings are easier to monitor than large portions left to spoil.

FEEDING SAFELY

Keeping their little world healthy.

Choose Safe Foods & Materials

Avoid pesticide-contaminated produce, chemically treated wood, spoiled supplemental foods, and heavily seasoned or salted human foods.

Introduce unfamiliar foods cautiously. Just because an isopod will eat something does not necessarily mean it belongs in its regular diet.

Prevent Excess Mold

Fungi are a natural part of decomposition, so small amounts of mold are not automatically a problem. However, heavy or persistent mold growth on supplemental food may indicate excessive feeding or unsuitable enclosure conditions.

Offer small portions, remove spoiled leftovers, and avoid leaving perishable foods in the enclosure for extended periods.

Check Moisture & Airflow

Persistently saturated substrate, stagnant air, and excessive food can encourage unwanted mold growth. Review ventilation and moisture conditions if mold becomes excessive.

Springtails can help consume some fungi and decomposing material, but they do not replace regular enclosure maintenance.

ONGOING ENCLOSURE CARE

Observe first. Adjust thoughtfully.

A healthy enclosure is a living environment that changes over time. Regularly check moisture levels, ventilation, food availability, and the condition of the substrate.

Replenish leaf litter and calcium when needed, remove spoiled food, and observe how your isopods use different areas of their habitat.

Make gradual, targeted adjustments rather than changing everything at once. If you suspect contamination or another immediate hazard, take prompt action to protect your colony.
